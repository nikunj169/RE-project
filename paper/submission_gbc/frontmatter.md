## Key Points {-}

- One estimator applied to observed DIC and tracer-based anthropogenic carbon at 342,277 GLODAP samples, with cruise offsets and drift removed
- Upper-ocean DIC accumulates 16–18 % faster than TRACE predicts, consistently across basins and matched by two eMLR(C\*) products
- Two Earth system models sampled like GLODAP confirm the estimators are unbiased; whether TRACE falls short in a model depends on the model

## Abstract {-}

Anthropogenic CO~2~ (C~ant~) in the ocean interior is inferred either from repeated measurements of dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC) or from transient-tracer age distributions, and the two are usually compared only as gridded inventories. We apply one within-location estimator to observed DIC and to tracer-based C~ant~ from TRACE at the same 342,277 GLODAPv2.2023 samples (1972–2021), conditioning on potential temperature, salinity and apparent oxygen utilisation. A differential estimator with cruise fixed effects is immune to any additive per-cruise offset, including offsets that drift with time. In the upper ocean (γ^n^ ≤ 27.0 kg m^−3^), DIC accumulated 16 % [11–21 %] faster than TRACE predicts, and 18 % [11–23 %] faster relative to the deep ocean once cruise offsets are removed. The excess is similar in all four basins, is matched at the same samples by two eMLR(C\*) products (13–20 %), and is not explained by uniform scale drift or by the choice of reference layers. In two Earth system models sampled at the same points, the estimators recover each model's exact C~ant~ rate. TRACE falls short of one model and slightly exceeds the other, so the models cannot independently confirm the shortfall. Replacing TRACE's damped surface CO~2~ boundary condition by the atmospheric record removes about 60 % of the observed excess. The deep Atlantic, by contrast, accumulates 25–35 % less carbon than the steady-state expectation. These results provide a drift-immune observational constraint on tracer-based C~ant~ accumulation rates.

## Plain Language Summary {-}

The ocean absorbs a large share of the carbon dioxide released by human activity. Because this extra carbon cannot be measured separately from the carbon naturally present in seawater, scientists estimate it in two ways: from repeated carbon measurements, or from the "age" of water derived from other chemical tracers. We compared the two directly, sample by sample, over fifty years of ship-based measurements, using a method that cannot be fooled by small calibration differences between research cruises. In the upper ocean, carbon has built up 16–18 % faster than the tracer-based method predicts. Two climate models, analysed in exactly the same way, confirm that our method measures the build-up correctly. They disagree, however, on whether the tracer method falls short, because each model circulates water differently from the real ocean. Part of the gap traces back to how the tracer method represents carbon dioxide at the sea surface. If confirmed, correcting this would raise estimates of how fast the upper ocean is storing human-made carbon.
